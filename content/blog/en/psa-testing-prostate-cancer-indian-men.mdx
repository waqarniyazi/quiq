## What PSA (Prostate-Specific Antigen) Measures

Prostate-Specific Antigen, or PSA, is a protein produced by cells in the prostate gland, a small organ located below a man's bladder. A small amount of PSA naturally circulates in the bloodstream. The test measures the level of this protein in your blood, reported as nanograms per millilitre (ng/mL). It's important to understand that an elevated PSA level is not a diagnosis of cancer. It's a signal—a biomarker that indicates something may be affecting the prostate.

Several benign (non-cancerous) conditions can raise PSA levels. The most common is benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), a natural enlargement of the prostate that occurs with age and can cause urinary symptoms. Prostatitis, an inflammation or infection of the prostate, can also cause a sharp, temporary rise in PSA. However, prostate cancer cells can produce more PSA, leading to elevated levels. Therefore, the PSA test acts as a crucial first-line screening tool. It tells you and your doctor that further investigation, such as a digital rectal exam (DRE) or a biopsy, might be warranted to determine the exact cause. Think of it not as a verdict, but as an early warning system for your prostate health.

## Why Early Detection Dramatically Improves Outcomes

The statistics speak for themselves. When prostate cancer is detected early—while it is still localized within the prostate gland—the 5-year survival rate is nearly 100%. This is the power of early detection. At this stage, treatment options are more effective, less invasive, and have far fewer side effects. Men have a wider range of choices, from active surveillance for slow-growing cancers to curative treatments like surgery or radiation.

The contrast with late-stage detection is stark. Once cancer spreads beyond the prostate to other parts of the body (metastasis), treatment becomes much more complex, focused on management rather than cure, and survival rates drop significantly. The goal of PSA screening is to find the cancer in the "window of curability." For Indian men, who may delay doctor visits due to busy schedules, cost concerns, or hesitation, this window can easily be missed. Regular screening from age 40 (or earlier if you have a family history) is the single most effective strategy to ensure you stay on the positive side of these statistics. It transforms a potentially life-threatening disease into a highly manageable one.
